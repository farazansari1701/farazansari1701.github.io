Why Is Life?

We spend our lives searching for meaning. But have we understood what we are trying to give meaning to?

Faraz AnsariPersonal perspectives

There is something rather strange about being alive.

We are born into a world we did not choose, given an existence we did not ask for, and spend much of that existence trying to understand what we are supposed to do with it.

We grow up believing that life must have a meaning.

But who told us that?

And why have we accepted it so readily?

We are taught to study, work, achieve, love, marry, raise children, build something, become someone and eventually leave something behind.

As though life is a question and our accomplishments are supposed to be the answer.

But what exactly are we answering?

Why do we spend so much of our lives trying to give meaning to something whose meaning we have never fully understood?

I have been thinking about the distinction between asking what the meaning of life is and asking why life exists in the first place.

One asks what we should do with our existence.

The other asks why there is an existence to do anything with.

Why is life?

It sounds almost grammatically incomplete.

But perhaps the question is incomplete because our understanding of existence is incomplete too.

The meaning we have been taught to pursue

I often wonder how much of what we call our purpose is genuinely our own.

A child is asked what they want to become when they grow up.

But why do we ask children what they want to become, as though being human is merely a temporary condition before they achieve something more important?

Why is becoming something treated as more meaningful than simply being someone?

We grow older and the expectations change.

Have you found a good job?

Are you earning enough?

When are you getting married?

When will you have children?

What comes next?

There always seems to be something next.

But where does this sequence lead?

Suppose I achieve everything I have been taught to want.

Would I finally understand why I exist?

Or would I simply have collected enough things to stop questioning it?

Would that be meaning?

Or would it be satisfaction?

And are the two necessarily the same?

What about someone who achieves none of these things?

Has that person failed at life?

If not, why do we continue measuring human existence through accomplishments?

Perhaps what troubles me is not that we search for meaning.

It is how rarely we question the meanings we have inherited.

Is meaning something we discover or something we invent?

Perhaps we do not discover meaning at all.

Perhaps we create it.

But if that is true, what makes one meaning more legitimate than another?

One person believes life is about love.

Another believes it is about power.

Someone finds meaning in faith, another in knowledge, another in wealth, and another in helping people they will never meet again.

Can all these meanings be equally valid?

If not, who decides?

And if meaning changes with experience, how can it be the final explanation for existence?

At one point, we might believe happiness lies in achieving a certain ambition.

Then we achieve it, and something changes.

We begin wanting something else.

Was our earlier purpose false?

Or simply temporary?

I wonder whether meaning exists independently of us, waiting to be understood, or whether it depends upon the person experiencing it.

If nobody existed to experience the universe, would the universe still have meaning?

And if meaning requires someone to recognise it, is consciousness the source of meaning?

Or merely the means through which we become aware of it?

I do not know.

Why do we need the universe to answer us?

Albert Camus explored the absurd through the tension between our longing for meaning and the world's failure to provide a clear answer.

But why do we assume the universe should answer us?

Does our ability to ask why mean that every why must have an answer we can understand?

Perhaps our difficulty is not the absence of meaning but our inability to tolerate uncertainty.

We want explanations.

For our existence.

For our suffering.

For the people we lose.

For the opportunities we never receive.

But would an explanation necessarily bring acceptance?

If we understood why suffering exists, would suffering become easier to bear?

Or would we simply begin questioning the explanation itself?

Perhaps knowledge and acceptance are entirely different things.

We understand that death is inevitable and still fear it.

We understand that people change and still struggle when they do.

We understand that nothing in this world lasts forever and continue becoming attached as though permanence were possible.

Why?

Is this contradiction something we are supposed to overcome?

Or is it part of what it means to be human?

The strange burden of existing

There is a couplet by Mirza Ghalib that seems particularly relevant:

نہ تھا کچھ تو خدا تھا، کچھ نہ ہوتا تو خدا ہوتا
ڈبویا مجھ کو ہونے نے، نہ ہوتا میں تو کیا ہوتا

Na tha kuchh to Khuda tha, kuchh na hota to Khuda hota,
Duboya mujh ko hone ne, na hota main to kya hota.

My rendering: When nothing existed, God was; even if nothing existed, God would remain. Existence itself has overwhelmed me. What would I have been had I never existed?

What does it mean to be overwhelmed by existence itself?

Is life a gift?

A responsibility?

A test?

A burden?

Or can it be all these things at once?

We celebrate the beginning of life, yet spend so much of it fearing its end.

We become attached to people knowing that separation is possible.

We make promises about a future whose arrival we cannot guarantee.

Why do we do this?

Is it because we forget that life is temporary?

Or because remembering it constantly would make living almost impossible?

Would love mean less if it lasted forever?

Does something become valuable because it is temporary?

And if not, why do we so often use mortality to explain why life matters?

Allah's answer to why we exist

As a Muslim, I cannot explore the question of existence without returning to what Allah says in the Qur'an.

And perhaps this is where my questioning becomes more complicated.

Because the Qur'an does not leave the purpose of human creation entirely unanswered.

In Surah Adh-Dhariyat (51:56), Allah says:

وَمَا خَلَقْتُ ٱلْجِنَّ وَٱلْإِنسَ إِلَّا لِيَعْبُدُونِ

“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me.”

And in Surah Al-Mulk (67:2):

ٱلَّذِى خَلَقَ ٱلْمَوْتَ وَٱلْحَيَوٰةَ لِيَبْلُوَكُمْ أَيُّكُمْ أَحْسَنُ عَمَلًا

“He is the One Who created death and life in order to test which of you is best in deeds.”

These verses offer something I have been searching for throughout this reflection.

An answer to what human life is for.

To worship Allah.

To be tested through our conduct.

Yet I find myself asking whether knowing the stated purpose of creation means that I have fully understood it.

What does worship truly mean?

Is it limited to the moments when we pray?

Or must it also be reflected in our honesty, our compassion, the way we treat other people and the decisions we make when nobody is watching?

If life is a test, what exactly is being tested?

Our faith?

Our intentions?

Our actions?

Our character?

And what happens when these do not align?

What if a person performs good deeds but seeks recognition?

What if someone sincerely wishes to do good but repeatedly fails to live according to their own principles?

What does it mean to be the best in deeds rather than merely the most accomplished?

And why do we spend so much time comparing our worldly achievements when the Qur'an directs our attention towards something different?

There is another verse that seems almost to challenge the very premise of my questioning.

In Surah Al-Mu'minun (23:115), Allah asks:

أَفَحَسِبْتُمْ أَنَّمَا خَلَقْنَاكُمْ عَبَثًا وَأَنَّكُمْ إِلَيْنَا لَا تُرْجَعُونَ

“Did you then think that We had created you without purpose, and that you would never be returned to Us?”

I find this verse particularly striking.

Because it is itself a question.

Allah does not merely state that human creation has purpose. The verse asks us to confront the assumption that we might have been created without one.

But does recognising that existence has purpose mean that I understand the entirety of that purpose?

And does knowing that I will return to Allah change the way I understand the life I am living now?

If I truly believe that my existence is temporary and that I am accountable for my actions, why do I sometimes behave as though worldly achievements are the ultimate measure of success?

Why do I become so concerned with things that may not matter as much as I imagine?

Why do I find it easier to speak about patience than to practise it?

Why is compassion so easy to admire and sometimes so difficult to demonstrate?

I do not ask these questions because I believe the Qur'an has failed to provide an answer.

I ask them because I wonder how deeply I have understood the answer already given.

Perhaps there is a difference between knowing why Allah created us and understanding how to live in accordance with that purpose.

And perhaps there is another distinction.

The Qur'an tells me what I was created for. But can I, with my limited human understanding, claim to comprehend the entirety of Allah's wisdom in creating existence?

Would that not itself require a humility I am still learning?

If life is a test, why are our tests so different?

I find myself returning to the differences between human lives.

Some people are born into security.

Others into hardship.

Some encounter love and care.

Others experience neglect.

Some have access to education, opportunity and freedom, while others spend their lives struggling for things many of us take for granted.

If life is a test, how should I understand these differences?

And more importantly, what responsibility do these differences place upon those of us who have been given certain opportunities?

Does having more make us more successful?

Or does it simply make us accountable for more?

Can someone who has very little live a more meaningful life than someone who possesses everything?

If so, why do we remain so eager to measure success through what a person owns?

Perhaps our understanding of success has become disconnected from the very purpose we claim to believe in.

But am I any different?

Do I measure my own progress through the things I achieve?

Do I become disappointed when life does not unfold according to my expectations?

And if I believe Allah has given existence a purpose, why do I sometimes allow my personal ambitions to become the measure of whether my life is going well?

I am not perfect.

I make mistakes. I question myself. There are principles I believe in that I do not always live up to.

I am a sinner, learning every day and trying to become better.

But perhaps this is precisely why the question matters.

What is the value of knowing life's purpose if I do not continually question whether my actions reflect it?

Can questioning itself be part of faith?

I wonder why questioning is sometimes treated as though it must be opposed to belief.

Can I believe in Allah and still question the world around me?

Can I accept that existence has purpose while acknowledging that I do not understand every aspect of it?

Does faith require the absence of uncertainty?

Or can faith involve recognising the limits of my own knowledge?

The Qur'an invites human beings to reflect upon creation.

But what does genuine reflection require?

Does it simply mean looking for evidence that confirms what we already believe?

Or must it also involve questioning our assumptions, recognising our limitations and becoming more conscious of our responsibilities?

I would like to think that faith should make me more humble, not more certain that I understand everything.

But even that raises another question.

How do I distinguish humility from avoiding difficult questions?

And how do I distinguish sincere questioning from an endless search that prevents me from acting upon what I already know?

Perhaps questioning and responsibility must exist together.

But I am still learning what that balance looks like.

Why is life?

I began by wondering why everyone seems so desperate to give meaning to life when we have never completely understood what life itself means.

But perhaps my question needs to change.

Because I cannot honestly say that my faith leaves me without an answer about the purpose of human creation.

The Qur'an tells me that I was created to worship Allah.

That life and death are a test.

That I was not created without purpose.

And that I will return to Him.

But does knowing these things mean I have understood existence?

Does knowing why I am here mean I understand why everything happens as it does?

Does knowing that life is a test mean I understand the wisdom behind every difficulty?

Does recognising that I will return to Allah mean I have truly understood the value of the time I have been given?

And if I have not, what would it take for that understanding to become more than words?

Perhaps I have been confusing the search for an answer with the responsibility of living according to one.

But even then, another question remains.

Why does Allah create a life in which we must learn, struggle, question, choose and eventually die?

How much of the wisdom behind creation can the human mind truly comprehend?

And should the limits of that comprehension trouble me?

Or should they remind me that I am not the author of existence, merely someone experiencing a small part of it?

I cannot answer every question about life.

Nor can I claim to understand the entirety of Allah's wisdom.

But I can question whether I am living in a way that reflects what I believe.

Whether I am becoming more compassionate.

More honest.

More conscious of my responsibilities.

More willing to acknowledge my mistakes.

And less convinced that my worldly achievements are the ultimate measure of my existence.

Still, I do not want to end by pretending that these thoughts have resolved everything.

Because even if I understand what I am supposed to live for, I remain conscious of how much I do not understand about life itself.

Allah has told us that we were not created without purpose.

But have I understood that purpose deeply enough to live according to it?

And if I have been given a life in which to discover, question and fulfil that purpose, how much of that life have I already spent looking for meaning in things that were never meant to define it?

Perhaps that is the question I ought to ask myself more often.

Not merely why life exists.

But why I sometimes live as though I have forgotten what I believe it exists for.

And perhaps the question does not end there.

If I know what I was created for, why is it still so difficult to become the person that knowledge asks me to be?

I do not know whether I will ever fully understand the wisdom of existence.

But I wonder whether understanding everything was ever what was asked of me in the first place.

  2. Qur'an — Surah Al-Mulk 67:2 — Life and death described as a test of deeds.
  3. Qur'an — Surah Al-Mu'minun 23:115 — A question challenging the assumption of purposeless creation.
