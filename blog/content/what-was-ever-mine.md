Ownership is a strange concept.

We spend much of our lives acquiring things, forming relationships and building a world around ourselves. Somewhere along the way, we begin describing almost everything through the language of possession.

My house. My land. My money.

My family. My friend. My wife. My husband. My children.

We use the same word for property and a human being.

*Mine.*

Of course, calling someone my friend does not ordinarily mean I consider them property. It may simply express affection or belonging. But I wonder how often that distinction becomes blurred.

When we say someone is ours, what exactly do we believe belongs to us? Their presence? Their affection? Their loyalty? Their decisions?

And if we have done something for them, does that strengthen our claim?

**Why do we sometimes behave as though emotional investment gives us a right to another person's life?**

## The road that outlived its owner

I remember noticing an inscription somewhere near Richmond. It referred to a person who had given land for the construction of a road centuries ago. I cannot remember the name or whether the date belonged to the seventeenth or eighteenth century.

But I remember questioning what I had read.

Someone had once owned that land. Perhaps they inherited or purchased it. Whatever the circumstances, it eventually became part of a road.

The earth existed before they were born. It continued to exist after they died. People use that road now without knowing anything about the person associated with it.

So what exactly did they own? The land itself, or the right to determine how it was used for a particular period?

Property rights are not meaningless. They protect people's homes, livelihoods and independence. Yet that inscription made me question the permanence we attach to ownership.

**If ownership is complicated when we speak about land, why do we apply its language so carelessly to human beings?**

Land cannot object to being called mine.

A human being can.

## When love begins to resemble ownership

I sometimes wonder how much of what we call love is love, and how much is our desire to feel secure in another person's attachment to us.

We become close to someone. We share experiences, secrets, difficulties and memories. Gradually, we develop expectations.

We want them to remain present, understand us, prioritise us and reassure us of our importance.

None of these desires is necessarily unreasonable. Relationships require care and consideration.

But where does expectation become entitlement?

Suppose I spend years loving someone. Does the duration of my affection oblige them to return it? If I make sacrifices, am I entitled to determine how they respond?

We sometimes say:

*I have done so much for you.*

*I was there when nobody else was.*

*After everything I sacrificed, this is how you treat me?*

Sometimes these words express genuine hurt. Someone may have broken a promise or betrayed trust. That matters.

But sometimes they carry another message: because I supported you, your future decisions must include my approval.

At what point has affection become a demand for control?

**If love must be returned because of what we invested, is it still love, or has it become a transaction?**

## The hidden invoice in kindness

Of all the forms of entitlement, the one I find most difficult to understand is believing that helping someone gives us authority over them.

Imagine supporting a friend during a difficult period. Years later, they make a decision you dislike.

Would your earlier help give you the right to interfere? Would their disagreement make them ungrateful, or would it simply mean they remain a separate human being?

I wonder whether some acts of kindness arrive with expectations never openly discussed.

We offer help. We call it unconditional. But perhaps we begin maintaining an account.

Every favour becomes a deposit. Every sacrifice becomes an investment. Every moment of support becomes evidence that the other person owes us something.

And when they fail to respond as hoped, we present them with a bill they never knew existed.

Gratitude matters. Reciprocity matters. Nobody should be expected to give endlessly while their own needs are ignored.

But appreciating someone's contribution is not the same as granting them authority over your life.

If I help someone, have I helped them become independent? Or have I created a reason for them to remain dependent upon me?

If I am disappointed when they no longer need me, was I hoping for their well-being, or my importance in their life?

## What the Qur'an says about giving without controlling

In Surah Al-Baqarah (2:262), Allah praises those who spend in His cause and do not follow their generosity with reminders or hurtful treatment.

The following verse, 2:263, says:

**قَوْلٌ مَّعْرُوفٌ وَمَغْفِرَةٌ خَيْرٌ مِّن صَدَقَةٍ يَتْبَعُهَا أَذًى**

*“Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury.”*

I find this deeply relevant.

The verses concern charity; I would not pretend every personal relationship is identical to charitable giving. But they make me question a broader habit.

Why do we sometimes believe that doing something good gives us permission to behave badly afterwards?

If I help someone and later use that help to humiliate them, have I relieved their difficulty, or replaced it with another?

Surah Al-Insan (76:8–9) describes people feeding those in need for the sake of Allah, seeking neither reward nor gratitude from them.

Would I still help someone who never thanked me? Would I wish them well if they became independent of me?

I cannot honestly claim I have never wanted recognition. Being forgotten can hurt.

Perhaps the distinction is not between feeling disappointment and feeling none. It is between experiencing disappointment and using it to justify control.

## Do parents own the lives they create?

Parents make extraordinary sacrifices for their children. They deserve gratitude, respect and care.

But does bringing someone into the world mean their life belongs to the people who brought them here?

*I raised you. I gave you everything. I sacrificed my life for you.*

These statements may be true.

But what should follow from them? Gratitude and responsibility, or unquestioning obedience?

If a child becomes an adult with different ambitions, beliefs or preferences, does that diminish what their parents have done?

In Surah Luqman (31:14), the Qur'an emphasises gratitude towards parents and acknowledges the hardship of motherhood. Yet the following verse, 31:15, describes parents pressuring a child to associate partners with Allah: the child must not obey that demand, while continuing to treat the parents with kindness.

The verse concerns a specific question of faith, not every family disagreement. But the distinction is worth considering.

Respect and agreement are not identical. Kindness and obedience are not always the same thing.

Perhaps love becomes troubling when sacrifices made for someone become a justification for denying them the freedom to become themselves.

## Can another person's emotions ever belong to us?

How does one own an emotion?

Suppose someone once loved me deeply. We shared thoughts, trust and imagined a future together. Then their feelings changed.

Would the love they once gave me mean I was entitled to keep receiving it? Would our memories create a permanent claim over their emotions?

I might feel hurt. I might struggle to understand what changed.

But would that make their heart mine?

Promises matter. Deception matters. People remain responsible for how they treat one another.

Yet can shared history compel someone to continue feeling what they no longer feel?

Nida Fazli wrote:

**ہر آدمی میں ہوتے ہیں دس بیس آدمی**  
**جس کو بھی دیکھنا ہو کئی بار دیکھنا**

*Har aadmi mein hote hain das bees aadmi,*  
*Jis ko bhi dekhna ho kai baar dekhna.*

*My rendering: There are many versions of a person within every human being. To truly know someone, look at them more than once.*

We are not fixed objects. We change.

Perhaps we become attached not only to a person, but to the version of them that once made us feel secure.

And when they change, we experience their development as a personal loss.

**Do we love the person, or the comfort of knowing they will always fulfil the role we assigned to them?**

## Marriage: belonging with someone or belonging to someone?

Marriage involves commitment, promises and responsibilities. Expecting honesty, fidelity and care is not the same as claiming ownership.

In Surah Ar-Rum (30:21), Allah describes tranquillity, affection and mercy between spouses as signs for those who reflect.

Mercy does not sound like possession. Affection need not require domination.

Yet when does concern become restriction? When does loyalty become obedience? When does protecting a relationship become controlling the person within it?

Surah An-Nisa (4:19) prohibits inheriting women against their will and instructs fair treatment. Its historical context is specific, but the rejection of treating women as inheritable possessions makes me question the possessive expectations that can survive in relationships.

Perhaps there is a difference between belonging *with* someone and belonging *to* someone.

One suggests companionship. The other, when taken literally, suggests ownership.

But freedom is not an excuse for dishonesty or neglecting commitments. The challenge is respecting another person's individuality while accepting the obligations of a shared life.

## The entitlement I may not recognise in myself

It would be easy to write about controlling people as though they were always someone else.

But what if I become irritated when someone does not reply as quickly as I expected? What if I make time for them and silently assume they should always be available for me?

Am I responding to a broken commitment, or an expectation I never discussed?

Sometimes hurt is justified. Sometimes it comes from another person exercising a freedom we quietly assumed they would surrender.

Human beings rarely experience themselves as controlling. We believe we are asking for respect, loyalty, appreciation or reassurance.

And sometimes we are.

But when does reassurance become a demand that someone continually prove their affection?

**Can I love someone deeply while accepting that I am not the centre of their existence?**

In Surah Al-Isra (17:70), Allah says He has honoured the children of Adam.

If another person possesses dignity granted by Allah, how could I justify treating their decisions as something I purchased through sacrifice?

I can establish boundaries. I can refuse a harmful relationship. I can expect honesty. But I cannot choose what another person must feel.

We can offer love without being promised its return. We can give someone an important place in our lives without acquiring the right to occupy the same place in theirs.

Why is that difficult to accept?

Perhaps because letting someone choose freely means accepting the possibility that they may not choose us.

## Possessing things without being possessed by them

There is another question I do not want to lose among my thoughts about people.

What about the things I hope to own?

I still want a comfortable life. I want security, independence and the freedom to enjoy what I work for. I do not believe that wanting these things makes me less concerned about living rightly.

Surah Al-Qasas (28:77) urges us to seek the Hereafter through what Allah has given us, without forgetting our share of this world, and to do good as Allah has done good to us.

What interests me is that the verse does not simply ask us to abandon worldly life.

It asks us to consider how we live within it.

Perhaps a home is not valuable only because I can call it mine, but because of the care and security it makes possible. Perhaps wealth is not wrong to desire, but it becomes troubling when acquiring more matters more than what we do with what we already have.

And perhaps relationships deserve an even greater humility.

I can work to purchase a house. I cannot work enough to purchase someone's affection.

I can give someone money, time or support. I cannot use those gifts to acquire their freedom.

**Am I using what I have been given to live well and help others live well, or am I allowing what I possess to decide who I become?**

## What was ever mine?

The road near Richmond will probably outlive many of the people who use it today.

But perhaps I was asking the wrong question about the land. Maybe what mattered was not whether it had truly belonged to someone, but what they chose to do with it while they could.

The same question applies to possessions, generosity and relationships.

I still want a home, comfort, companionship and the possibility of sharing my life with someone I love.

But can I pursue these things without believing that acquiring them gives me authority over them?

Can I love without possessing? Give without keeping an account? Care without controlling? Be disappointed without turning disappointment into a demand?

Some things can be possessed. Some things can only be shared.

Perhaps the troubling part of ownership is not the claim we make over objects, but the claim we sometimes make over people.

Land cannot say that it feels controlled. Money cannot say that it has been humiliated.

A person can.

And sometimes, instead of listening, we remind them of everything we have done for them.

As though kindness were a contract they unknowingly signed. As though love were a debt. As though sacrifice were evidence of ownership.

**Perhaps the measure of love is not how much of another person we can claim as ours, but how much of their humanity we respect when it does not serve our wishes.**

If I do something good for someone, and then expect their life to unfold according to my desires, was I truly helping them?

Or was I trying to acquire something that was never mine to own?

And if I claim to love someone but cannot accept that they may choose differently from what I want, am I loving them?

**Or merely loving the idea that they belong to me?**
