It has been a busy, mentally exhausting day, and I cannot reduce it to one clean emotion. An interaction stayed with me. So did questions about society and politics, and the uncomfortable possibility that intentions can be misunderstood. I have no interest in turning another person into a character in an essay. What I want to examine is something less easily settled: the distance between speaking and being understood.

That distance exists between people. It also exists between people and the countries that claim to speak for them.

I found myself thinking about languages: Hindi, Urdu, English and Spanish, and, beyond them, French. They travel with people, but they also travel with power. A language can hold childhood, humour, affection and the ordinary business of life. It can also be made into a requirement, a test of loyalty, or a dividing line between those who are said to belong and those expected to adjust.

My starting thought was that the spread of Hindi in India and Urdu in Pakistan cannot be separated from nationalism. I am especially troubled by the prospect of a common language being elevated above the older languages of regions such as Balochistan and Kashmir, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka and Telangana. I think of what happens when the state tells people which language should express their identity, rather than asking how they already speak, remember and understand the world.

Yet I need to be careful with the history, including the parts that complicate my own argument.

Hindi and Urdu were not created by the border drawn in 1947. Their shared everyday linguistic foundations developed over centuries, and Urdu’s history belongs to the cultural world of northern India, including Delhi, as well as the Deccan and other places. The political and literary distinction between standard Hindi and standard Urdu grew sharper before Partition, with different scripts, vocabularies and public associations acquiring increasing significance. Religion and politics helped shape those associations, but neither language belongs exclusively to a single faith or nation.

India did not simply invent Hindi to distinguish itself from Pakistan. Hindi became the official language of the Indian Union, while English continued to be used for Union purposes, and other languages retained constitutional recognition. Pakistan adopted Urdu as its national language, although it was not the mother tongue of most Pakistanis. These are not identical choices, and they did not begin from identical circumstances. Still, I cannot ignore their shared question: what happens when national unity is imagined in one language, while citizens live in many?

I understand the appeal of a shared language. People need to learn together, participate in public life and communicate across regions. A common language can open doors. I do not want to confuse every attempt at mutual understanding with coercion. But I hesitate when a bridge becomes a gate, when access to public life depends on leaving another language at its entrance.

The history of what is now Bangladesh makes the cost of that confusion difficult to dismiss. In 1952, students in East Pakistan were killed while demanding recognition for Bengali. That history does not make every language policy equally oppressive, but it shows that the language people use is not a minor administrative detail. It can be inseparable from dignity and political recognition.

I also think about the languages associated with empires. English, French and Spanish spread across continents through histories that included colonial conquest, government and schooling. Those histories differed, and so did the people living under them. It would be wrong to say that every person speaking these languages today speaks only in the voice of an empire. People have made these languages their own. They have used them to challenge authority, to write new literatures, to love and to demand rights. A language may arrive with power and then become the means by which power is questioned.

That contradiction matters to me. I do not want to romanticise regional languages as though they never changed, or treat a language spoken across borders as though it has no genuine emotional home. My concern is with the hierarchy we create between voices: the assumption that one way of speaking is sophisticated, another merely local; one suitable for government, another fit only for home. The difference between being multilingual and being required to abandon something is not small.

James Baldwin, writing about Black English in an American context with its own distinct history, saw language as bound up with the experience and identity of its speakers. His argument raises a question I cannot easily leave behind: when we dismiss the way people speak, are we sometimes dismissing the lives that gave rise to their words? The histories are not interchangeable. The warning is still worth hearing.

And there is a brief line from Faiz Ahmad Faiz’s Urdu nazm *Bol*:

**بول کہ لب آزاد ہیں تیرے**

*Bol ke lab azaad hain tere* — “Speak, for your lips are free” (my translation).

It is an appeal to expression, not an answer to every argument about national language. I find the distinction important. Freedom to speak is not the same as a guarantee of being heard. Nor does the right to my voice remove my responsibility to listen when another person’s experience challenges it.

Perhaps that is why today’s questions about friendship, family responsibilities and generational expectations sit beside my thoughts about history. In a country, a family or a friendship, we can mistake familiarity for understanding. We assume that sharing words means sharing meanings. We assume that good intentions will be obvious to the person receiving them. They are not always obvious. Sometimes another person hears a demand where we intended an invitation, or hears distance where we were trying to make room. I cannot claim that this explains any particular interaction today. It is simply a possibility worth taking seriously.

My thoughts have also returned to home, to my connection with India, and to the way a person’s identity changes with time. Memory can make a place feel close without making belonging straightforward. I do not think I have to choose between caring for a culture and questioning some of its traditions. Nor should disagreement about a tradition automatically be mistaken for rejection of every person who lives within it. Generations may want different things from freedom, and they may attach different meanings to respect.

I am still working out where responsibility begins. I believe people should be free to express opinions and shape their own lives; I also know that words have consequences for others. Those commitments need not cancel each other. Perhaps responsibility is not the instruction to remain silent, but the effort to speak without deciding in advance that every disagreement is disloyalty.

This is where nationalism unsettles me most. I can understand attachment to a country, a language, a shared history. What I question is the moment attachment becomes a measure used against someone else. A nation can recognise a common story without pretending that it contains only one tongue, one memory or one kind of citizen.

There was room today, too, for something quieter: gratitude or relief. I will not give it a story I have not told. I only want to leave space for it beside the fatigue and the questions.

Can people change the way they listen? Can societies learn to treat difference as something other than a threat? Can I challenge an idea without ceasing to examine my own? I have no tidy answers tonight. I am left thinking that a border may determine where a government ends, but it cannot, by itself, tell us where a language belongs. And understanding another person may begin at the very point where the certainty of our own words gives way.
