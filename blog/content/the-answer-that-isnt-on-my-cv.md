An application form asks for many things.

Your name. Your experience. Your qualifications. Your achievements. Your salary expectations. Whether you have the right to work in the country.

It asks you to summarise years of your life in carefully structured boxes, as though everything that makes you suitable for an opportunity can be explained in a few hundred words.

What it never asks is what that opportunity actually means to you.

Perhaps because the answer would be too complicated.

I have been living in Britain since 2019. I came here to study, found my way into professional life, and gradually built something that has become much more than an overseas experience.

The United Kingdom is no longer simply a country I moved to. It is where much of my adult life has taken shape.

And yet, there are moments when the future of that life feels strangely dependent on decisions that are not entirely mine to make.

I work in digital marketing, a profession that constantly asks us to improve, adapt and understand change. Over the years, I have developed my skills, learnt new technologies and acquired experiences that have shaped how I approach problems.

But as I think about the next stage of my career, I am reminded that professional experience alone does not always determine which opportunities are possible.

For me, the question of employment is also connected to visa sponsorship.

It is a practical requirement. But practical requirements have emotional consequences, even when we do not speak about them.

There is a difference between asking whether a role would be right for you and asking whether that role could allow you to continue building your life in the place you have come to call home.

And that difference is not always easy to explain.

## When opportunities become more than opportunities

We often speak about careers in the language of ambition.

What position do you want next? What salary are you aiming for? Where do you see yourself in five years?

These are reasonable questions.

But sometimes, a career decision is not simply about progression.

Sometimes it represents continuity.

The ability to keep working, to fulfil responsibilities, to plan beyond the next few months, and to feel that the future is something you can reasonably prepare for.

I have been exploring professional opportunities and thinking carefully about what comes next.

That process has made me consider something I do not think we discuss enough: the emotional weight of depending on circumstances that effort alone cannot resolve.

You can prepare thoroughly, demonstrate your experience and find an opportunity that seems appropriate, only to discover that other requirements make it unsuitable.

You can possess the skills a role demands without meeting every condition attached to it.

That distinction matters.

It does not mean the experience you have accumulated has become less valuable.

It means that ability and opportunity, however closely related, are not the same thing.

And perhaps one of the more difficult lessons of adulthood is accepting that doing everything within your control does not give you control over everything that follows.

## A thousand wishes, and the ones that matter

I often find Urdu poetry remarkably precise in describing feelings that ordinary language struggles to contain.

Mirza Ghalib wrote:

ہزاروں خواہشیں ایسی کہ ہر خواہش پہ دم نکلے

بہت نکلے مرے ارمان لیکن پھر بھی کم نکلے

*Hazāroñ khvāhisheñ aisī ki har khvāhish pe dam nikle,*

*Bahut nikle mire armān lekin phir bhī kam nikle.*

My own English rendering:

*A thousand desires, each powerful enough to take my breath; so many wishes fulfilled, yet somehow still too few.*

Ghalib's ghazal is not about job applications, employment or the particular uncertainties of modern professional life.

Its exploration of human longing is much larger than that.

But the couplet makes me think about the complicated relationship between ambition and contentment.

We are encouraged to want more.

More knowledge. More security. More financial independence. More opportunities. More experiences.

And there is nothing inherently wrong with those desires.

Yet I wonder how easily we begin to treat the next achievement as the thing that will finally allow us to feel at peace.

Perhaps the real difficulty is not wanting too much.

Perhaps it is becoming so occupied with what we are waiting to achieve that we overlook what we have already managed to build.

I sometimes have to remind myself that the years behind me are not insignificant simply because the years ahead remain uncertain.

I have studied, worked, learnt, made decisions and navigated a life away from where I grew up.

None of that guarantees what happens next.

But neither does an uncertain future erase the value of what has already happened.

## The difference between effort and control

I believe in making an effort.

Not because I imagine hard work is a magical solution to every difficulty, but because I cannot reasonably expect my circumstances to change if I refuse to participate in shaping them.

At the same time, I am increasingly conscious of the limits of effort.

There are things we can influence through preparation, commitment and learning.

And there are things that depend on decisions, institutions, policies and circumstances beyond us.

I find this distinction important when I reflect on Islam.

In Surah An-Najm (53:39), the Qur'an draws attention to what a person strives towards. The surrounding verses concern moral accountability before Allah, rather than promising that every worldly effort will produce the result we want.

I do not want to turn a verse about accountability into a guarantee that submitting another application will lead to a successful outcome.

For me, the reflection is more personal.

I am responsible for what I do with the opportunities and abilities available to me.

I am responsible for being honest, for continuing to learn, for treating people fairly and for recognising where I have fallen short.

But responsibility does not mean I can determine every outcome.

I am not perfect. I make mistakes. I sometimes struggle with uncertainty and with the distance between the things I believe and the way I manage to live.

My faith does not remove those struggles.

It gives me reasons to question how I respond to them.

Am I allowing uncertainty to make me impatient?

Am I comparing my progress with people whose circumstances are entirely different?

Am I becoming so concerned with achieving something that I forget why I wanted it in the first place?

And am I able to accept what remains outside my control without abandoning the responsibility to keep trying?

I do not have perfect answers.

But I think they are worth asking.

## There is a person behind the application

Something about the way modern professional life measures people troubles me.

We are often required to present ourselves through concise achievements, defined competencies and evidence that our experience satisfies a particular requirement.

That is understandable. Employers need ways to evaluate candidates.

But I wonder whether we sometimes forget how much remains outside those documents.

A CV can describe what someone has done.

It cannot fully explain what they overcame to do it.

It cannot tell you how much uncertainty accompanied their decisions, what responsibilities they carry outside working hours, or what a particular opportunity might change in their life.

And it certainly cannot measure the entirety of their character.

I think this becomes particularly important for people whose professional lives involve moving between countries, systems and cultures.

There is a tendency to describe the experience of living abroad through its achievements.

Education. Employment. Independence. Career progression.

We rarely talk with equal openness about the administrative uncertainty, the adjustments, or the difficulty of making long-term decisions when important conditions are subject to external approval.

I do not say this to suggest that my experience is exceptional.

It is precisely because these experiences are shared by so many people that I think they deserve to be understood.

Behind what appears to be an ordinary professional decision, someone may be considering an entire life.

That deserves a little more humanity than a form can provide.

## I do not want to spend my life waiting to live

There is another danger in all this.

When an important part of the future feels uncertain, it becomes tempting to postpone the present.

To think that peace will arrive after the next decision.

That happiness can wait until the practical difficulties are resolved.

That life will begin properly once the next professional or personal milestone has been achieved.

But I have already spent years living a life that once existed only as a possibility.

The person who came to Britain in 2019 could not have known everything those years would bring.

And the person I am now cannot know everything the next few years will contain.

Perhaps neither version of me needs to know.

I want to be ambitious without becoming consumed by ambition.

To recognise the seriousness of my responsibilities without letting every uncertainty occupy every part of my thinking.

To continue seeking opportunities without making my sense of worth entirely dependent on whether one becomes available.

And to remember that the life I am trying to protect and develop is also the life happening in the present.

There are things I would like to achieve.

There are questions about work, security and the future that still need practical answers.

And there are decisions ahead whose outcomes I cannot predict.

I would be pretending if I said those things did not matter.

They matter enormously.

But I do not want uncertainty to convince me that everything else must stand still.

I can continue learning while waiting for clarity.

I can care about people while dealing with responsibilities.

I can appreciate the present without abandoning my hopes for the future.

And I can remain grateful for what I have experienced while recognising that there are things I still need to change.

Perhaps none of that will make the difficult decisions easier.

But it may prevent those decisions from becoming the only way I understand myself.

The next time an application asks me to summarise who I am, I will do my best to answer the question properly.

I will describe my experience, my abilities and what I hope to contribute.

And then I will remember that the answer is necessarily incomplete.

There is a person behind those words.

A person who has moved countries, built a working life, made mistakes, continued learning and carried ambitions that cannot all be reduced to a job title.

Someone who is still trying to understand what a good life should look like.

Someone who has not finished becoming himself.

An application may determine whether a particular door opens.

But it cannot determine the full worth of the person standing outside it.

And I think that is something I need to remember.
