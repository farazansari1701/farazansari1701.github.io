There is something rather strange about the certainty with which we speak about marriage.

Someone reaches a certain age, finds a suitable person, makes arrangements, and begins preparing for a future that is supposed to last a lifetime.

Rarely do we ask why.

Lately, I have found myself thinking about the different reasons people around me give for getting married. Some are already married. Others are preparing to marry. A few are still searching for someone with whom they might eventually share their lives.

And the more I consider their reasons, the more I wonder whether marriage is always a decision people consciously make, or sometimes simply the next thing they have been taught to do.

But I do find it curious that we can spend years deciding what to study, where to work and how to build a career, yet sometimes approach the decision to share an entire life with another person as though its necessity has already been established.

Perhaps the question is not whether marriage is a good thing.

Perhaps it is whether we understand what we are asking it to give us.

## The reasons we learn to give

I have friends who married because they had reached an age when marriage seemed appropriate.

Others wanted financial security. Some considered the comfort of two incomes and the stability that might offer. Some hoped to have children. Others were responding to expectations from their families or the society around them.

I have also encountered attitudes in which gifts, dowry and material benefits become part of the attraction.

There is nothing inherently wrong with wanting stability. Two people may quite reasonably decide that sharing financial responsibilities will allow them to build a more secure life. Likewise, wanting children or valuing one's family's wishes can be sincere and meaningful.

But I wonder what happens when these considerations become the entire explanation for choosing someone.

If I marry because another person can provide for me, what happens when they can no longer provide in the same way?

And if material benefits become the principal motivation, at what point does the relationship begin to resemble an arrangement in which the value of a person is measured by what they bring?

There is an important difference between traditions through which families express generosity and expectations that turn another human being into a financial opportunity.

The desire to have children raises a different question.

Becoming a parent and becoming a partner are two responsibilities that may overlap, but they are not identical. Someone may sincerely wish to raise a child without having considered what it means to understand, respect and care for the person with whom they intend to raise that child.

Then there is age.

We speak of being ready for marriage as though readiness arrives naturally with another birthday.

But does growing older necessarily make us more compassionate? More patient? More capable of handling disagreement or another person's vulnerability?

What troubles me is the possibility that some may never have considered whether the timetable belongs to them.

## When we say we marry for love

Of all these reasons, the one I have heard comparatively little about, at least among the people I have encountered, is love itself.

Perhaps that says more about the conversations I have been part of than about marriage generally. People may find it easier to discuss practical arrangements than private feelings.

What exactly is love?

We often describe it as something unconditional. We say that to love someone is to accept them for who they are.

Yet we enter relationships with expectations.

We want affection, understanding, honesty, loyalty, emotional safety and companionship. We hope to be supported through difficulties and respected when we disagree.

But if our love depends upon them, can we still call it unconditional?

Mirza Ghalib expresses something of love's resistance to human control:

**عشق پر زور نہیں ہے یہ وہ آتش غالبؔ**  
**کہ لگائے نہ لگے اور بجھائے نہ بنے**

*Ishq par zor nahin hai ye woh aatish, Ghalib,*  
*Ki lagaaye na lage aur bujhaaye na bane.*

*My rendering: Love is a fire that cannot be lit merely because we wish it, nor extinguished simply because we decide that it should end.*

We cannot necessarily command ourselves to love another person. Nor can we guarantee that a feeling, once present, will always remain unchanged.

Perhaps this is why I find Erich Fromm's understanding of love interesting. In *The Art of Loving*, he treats love not merely as a feeling but as something requiring care, responsibility, respect and knowledge.

A feeling may bring two people together. But perhaps what they do with that feeling determines whether they can build a life around it.

If someone repeatedly disregards our dignity, are we obliged to remain because we once promised to love them?

I do not think loving another person must mean surrendering every boundary.

It may be possible to care deeply for someone while recognising that remaining together would cause continued harm.

And perhaps the ability to walk away from a relationship does not always prove that the love within it was false.

When we say we love someone, do we love the person themselves?

Or do we love the way they make us feel about ourselves?

Perhaps we love being understood. Perhaps we love being needed, appreciated or reassured. Perhaps another person offers us a version of ourselves that we find easier to admire.

Those feelings need not make love insincere.

But what happens when that person can no longer offer us the same reassurance?

Do we continue loving them?

Or do we begin mourning the version of ourselves we experienced in their presence?

## The lives we remember differently

I sometimes wonder whether relationships are sustained as much by memory as by love.

There are ordinary moments that acquire meaning precisely because they were experienced together. A conversation remembered years later. An act of kindness during difficulty. A disagreement eventually resolved. The comfort of knowing that someone was present when life felt uncertain.

Yet memory can also work against us.

If years together have been marked by resentment, humiliation, neglect or repeated disappointment, the past may become something painful to carry.

And I wonder whether two people can spend the same years together while remembering almost entirely different relationships.

One might remember the laughter.

The other might remember how much effort it took to keep everyone happy.

One might recall the occasions when they were together.

The other might remember feeling alone in those very moments.

Neither person necessarily has complete access to the other's experience.

That makes the question of staying together particularly complicated.

Do we remain because we still love the person before us?

Or because we remember loving who they once were?

And if a relationship has lasted twenty or thirty years, does its duration necessarily tell us anything about the happiness of the people within it?

I would like to believe that meaningful memories can offer strength. But I am less convinced that nostalgia should be allowed to make decisions on our behalf.

Perhaps the difficulty is that leaving a relationship can sometimes feel like abandoning not only another person but also the years we have given to them.

Yet those years do not become meaningless simply because a relationship changes or ends.

They remain part of a life that continues to have value beyond the relationship itself.

## The difference between wanting someone and needing not to be alone

Another possibility troubles me.

What if some of us seek marriage not because we have found someone whose existence we genuinely wish to share, but because we are frightened of being alone?

I do not consider loneliness a trivial fear.

Human beings need connection. We want to be heard, understood, remembered and cared for. There is nothing shameful about longing for companionship.

But I wonder whether we sometimes place upon marriage the responsibility of solving problems that exist beyond the absence of a partner.

A person may have a spouse, a home and a family, and still feel profoundly lonely.

Another may remain unmarried and experience friendship, purpose, belonging and considerable peace.

Perhaps loneliness is not simply the absence of people.

Perhaps it is the absence of a connection in which we feel genuinely recognised.

I find Khalil Gibran's reflections in *The Prophet* particularly relevant here. Writing about marriage, he asks that there be space within togetherness.

His words do not suggest that companionship is unimportant. Rather, they make me consider whether closeness requires two people to surrender their individuality.

Can I choose someone's company without making them responsible for every uncertainty within me?

There is a difference between needing human connection and placing the whole burden of our happiness upon one particular human being.

Perhaps the distinction lies in whether we recognise their freedom when they are part of our lives.

## The strange timetable of being alive

The more I think about marriage, the less certain I am that marriage is the only subject I am questioning.

We grow up preparing for education. We study to prepare for employment. We work towards stability. We establish ourselves and begin thinking about marriage, children, a home, and eventually retirement.

There is nothing wrong with any of these ambitions.

But I increasingly wonder whether we become so occupied preparing for life that we forget we are already living it.

When will you settle down?

When will you marry?

When will you have children?

And when the expected order is interrupted, we sometimes describe the person as falling behind.

Behind whom?

What does it mean to be late in a life whose length none of us knows?

Allama Iqbal offers a couplet that feels relevant to this question of self-understanding:

**اپنے من میں ڈوب کر پا جا سراغ زندگی**  
**تو اگر میرا نہیں بنتا نہ بن اپنا تو بن**

*Apne man mein doob kar pa ja suraagh-e-zindagi,*  
*Tu agar mera nahin banta, na ban, apna to ban.*

*My rendering: Look within yourself and discover the meaning of life. If you cannot belong to me, then at least learn to become your own.*

What does it mean to become our own person?

Surely it cannot mean living without regard for others. Freedom does not excuse selfishness, nor does independence remove responsibility.

But perhaps it requires us to recognise which desires genuinely belong to us and which we have inherited without examining.

And I wonder whether we occasionally confuse achieving an expected life with discovering a meaningful one.

## What if what we want is not what we need?

The person we choose today is someone we hope to know tomorrow, although neither of us can be entirely certain who we will become.

And yet, marriage asks two people to make promises about a future neither can properly imagine.

How confidently can we decide what will make us happy for the rest of our lives?

Something that appears desirable today may prove unsuitable tomorrow. A disappointment may eventually reveal possibilities we had not considered. Something we once feared losing may turn out not to have been as essential as we believed.

But I am reluctant to turn this uncertainty into the comforting claim that everything happens for the best.

I do not know that.

Suffering is not automatically a lesson, and disappointment does not always conceal a blessing. There are experiences whose pain should not be explained away merely because we hope to find meaning afterwards.

Faith, for me, does not require pretending to understand every event.

In Surah Ar-Rum (30:21), the Qur'an speaks of spouses in connection with tranquillity, affection and mercy. The verse also invites reflection upon these signs.

What would it mean to build a relationship in which two people genuinely offered one another peace?

Perhaps simply the willingness to treat another person's vulnerability with care, particularly when doing so is difficult.

And if a marriage intended to offer tranquillity becomes a source of constant anxiety, should we not be willing to ask what has gone wrong?

I do not ask this as someone who has mastered the responsibilities of love.

I am imperfect. I make mistakes. I am still learning.

If questioning society's expectations teaches me anything, I hope it also teaches me to examine the expectations I place upon others.

Because it would be rather convenient to criticise the conditions under which people choose marriage while overlooking the conditions I might impose upon someone I claim to love.

## Before we promise forever

I do not want these questions to suggest that marriage is something to be avoided.

There are people who find extraordinary companionship within it. People who choose each other sincerely, who make sacrifices without keeping accounts of them, and who continue offering care when circumstances become difficult.

There may also be people who enter marriage for practical reasons and gradually discover a love deeper than they originally expected.

But I also think we owe ourselves a little more honesty about why we enter relationships in the first place.

Are we searching for someone to love, or someone who can make our lives appear complete?

Are we looking for companionship, or reassurance that we have not fallen behind?

Do we want a particular person, or merely the future we imagine that person will make possible?

I cannot answer these questions for anyone else.

I am not even certain that I have finished examining them within myself.

Perhaps some of my understanding will change with time. Perhaps experiences I have not yet had will challenge the conclusions I find reasonable today.

I would like to remain open to that possibility.

Because I do not think questioning marriage means losing faith in love.

Perhaps it means taking love seriously enough to resist treating another human being as a solution to an expectation, a fear or an unfinished ambition.

There is something profoundly uncertain about promising a lifetime to another person.

Neither person knows everything the future will demand of them. Neither can guarantee that they will always understand the other perfectly.

And yet, perhaps two people can recognise those limitations and still choose one another with sincerity.

Not because the world has decided it is time.

Not because a particular arrangement promises to remove every difficulty.

But because, having considered the uncertainties, they genuinely wish to share the life that unfolds.

I do not know whether that is enough.

I do not know whether any of us can completely understand the reasons behind a decision so deeply connected to hope, fear, love and the desire to belong.

But I wonder whether we should spend a little less time asking people when they intend to marry, and a little more time considering whether they have had the freedom to understand why.

After all, there is a remarkable confidence in the word *forever*.

Especially when it is spoken by human beings who cannot be certain of tomorrow.

And perhaps that is the question I am left with tonight.

**Before we ask another person to promise us a lifetime, have we understood what we are hoping that promise will give us?**
