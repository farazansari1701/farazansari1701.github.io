The Years That Count

The future remains a possibility. The present is the part of life we are actually living.

Faraz AnsariPersonal perspectives

There is something deeply unsettling about realising that the future you have spent years working towards may not exist in the form you imagined.

Not because you stopped trying. Not because your ambitions were unreasonable. But because life has a peculiar way of changing the circumstances upon which our expectations depend.

Today, I found myself thinking about the last seven years of my life.

Seven years is long enough to become someone different, to build a career, develop friendships, experience disappointment, discover new ambitions and begin calling an unfamiliar place home. Yet sometimes even seven years are not enough to know what the next few years will look like.

I came to the United Kingdom in 2019, carrying the expectations that many people have when they leave home in search of something better. I wanted to study, establish myself professionally, learn, grow and eventually create a life stable enough to call my own.

There was always an imagined sequence. Education would lead to a career. A career would eventually provide stability. Stability would make other decisions possible. And somewhere along that journey, perhaps I would find someone with whom I could share the rest of it.

Marriage, a home, children, a family.

Not necessarily in that order, and certainly not according to a rigid timetable. But they belonged to the future I believed I was gradually approaching.

Recent uncertainty surrounding immigration and settlement has made me question that imagined future. More importantly, it has made me question why we attach so much of our happiness to a tomorrow that has never promised to arrive as expected.

The future we keep rehearsing

I sometimes wonder whether human beings spend more time imagining life than actually living it.

During childhood, we prepare for adulthood. During education, we prepare for employment. Once employed, we prepare for a better position, greater independence and a place of our own. Every achievement brings another expectation.

We tell ourselves that life will feel complete when certain circumstances align: when we have found the right person, when we are married, when we have children, when we finally have somewhere to call home.

But is there ever a moment when everything genuinely aligns?

Or do we move from one uncertainty to another, convincing ourselves that the next achievement will finally bring peace?

Uncertainty rarely announces itself as something extraordinary. Sometimes it arrives as a change in policy. Sometimes as an unexpected conversation, an opportunity that disappears, a relationship that does not develop as imagined, or a decision we must suddenly reconsider.

One day the future seems clear. The next, we are rearranging years of expectations.

Nothing about the past has changed. The work was still done. The sacrifices were still made. The years were still lived. Only our understanding of where they were leading has changed.

The uncertainty of finding someone

I have been thinking about how much of love depends on timing.

We speak of finding the right person as though compatibility alone were enough. But what happens when two people meet at the wrong time? Or when their circumstances make the future difficult to imagine?

What does it mean to promise someone a shared life when you are uncertain about the foundations upon which that life might be built?

I sometimes wonder whether we seek a partner because we are ready to share our lives, or because we hope another person will make life itself feel less uncertain.

There is a difference.

To want companionship is human. To wish for someone who understands your thoughts, shares your silences and remains present through ordinary difficulties is hardly unreasonable.

But perhaps it is unfair to expect another person to resolve uncertainties we have not learned to live with ourselves.

A partner cannot guarantee that life will unfold according to our expectations. They cannot promise favourable circumstances, professional success or an explanation for every disappointment. Neither can we.

Perhaps the deeper question is not whether two people can imagine a perfect future together, but whether they can accept that the future will remain imperfect and still choose to face it together.

There is a difference between loving the idea of someone who fits our plans and loving an actual person whose fears, ambitions and uncertainties may complicate those plans.

The first asks whether someone belongs in the future we have imagined. The second asks whether we are willing to imagine a future that includes someone else's reality.

How long should one postpone the possibility of love while waiting to become sufficiently settled to deserve it?

At what point does wanting to be responsible prevent us from experiencing the very things for which we are trying to become responsible?

I do not have an answer.

The family that may follow

I sometimes imagine what it might mean to become a father.

Not simply the responsibility of caring for a child, but the extraordinary possibility that another person might one day look towards me for reassurance about a world I do not fully understand myself.

What would I tell them about life?

Would I encourage them to dream boldly, knowing dreams can be interrupted? Would I teach them to plan carefully, while explaining that careful planning cannot prevent disappointment?

Would I tell them that hard work is always rewarded, when experience has taught me that effort and outcomes are not necessarily proportional?

Or would I teach them something more difficult: that life is uncertain, goodness does not guarantee ease, and a person's value cannot be measured only through achievements?

Perhaps fatherhood is not about possessing all the answers. Perhaps it is about learning to live with questions without passing every fear on to the next generation.

We want to become established before we marry, to find a home before having children, to feel certain before making commitments that could shape several lives rather than just our own.

These desires come from a reasonable place. But if certainty is the condition upon which we allow ourselves to begin living, what happens when certainty never comes?

This is not an argument for disregarding responsibility. It is a question about whether the search for perfect circumstances can sometimes become another way of avoiding the vulnerability meaningful commitments require.

No parent can guarantee a difficulty-free life. No husband or wife can promise that marriage will be untouched by uncertainty.

Perhaps what matters is the care, honesty and responsibility with which people face what they cannot control.

The impossibility of a complete world

There is a couplet by Nida Fazli that feels particularly appropriate:

کبھی کسی کو مکمل جہاں نہیں ملتا
کہیں زمین کہیں آسماں نہیں ملتا

Kabhi kisi ko mukammal jahaan nahin milta,
Kahin zameen kahin aasmaan nahin milta.

My rendering: No one is given an entirely complete world. Somewhere, something of the earth is missing; elsewhere, something of the sky.

We imagine completeness as a destination: a meaningful career, a loving partner, a stable home, children, a life without any obvious absence.

But perhaps such a life exists more comfortably in imagination than in reality.

One person may find professional recognition but struggle to find companionship. Another may have love but lack stability. Someone may have everything they once believed they wanted and still experience an emptiness they cannot explain.

We see other people's achievements without their sacrifices, relationships without their difficulties, happiness without the questions beneath it.

Perhaps the couplet reminds us that life must be recognised as meaningful without demanding that it first become complete.

Because if every achievement simply reveals another absence, when exactly do we allow ourselves to be content?

What is life actually about?

Is life about achieving the things we once imagined? Accumulating experiences, finding love, raising children, fulfilling responsibilities, or leaving evidence that we existed?

If circumstances prevent us from achieving the life we planned, does that make the life we actually lived less meaningful?

I doubt existence can be reduced to a sequence of accomplishments.

Perhaps life is not a problem that can be permanently solved. Perhaps it is an experience that must be continuously understood, questioned and lived.

The human condition is almost paradoxical. We know life is temporary, yet pursue permanence. We know change is inevitable, yet make plans that depend on things remaining unchanged.

We know people, opportunities and circumstances may leave us, yet continue allowing ourselves to become attached.

Perhaps this attachment is part of what makes life meaningful: the willingness to care deeply about something we cannot guarantee we will keep.

To love someone despite knowing neither person will remain unchanged. To raise a child despite knowing we cannot control the world they will inherit. To make an effort despite knowing the outcome is not promised.

Maybe meaning is found less in the certainty of possessing something and more in the sincerity with which we choose to care for it.

The illusion of holding the reins

Mirza Ghalib expresses our helplessness before time:

رو میں ہے رخشِ عمر کہاں دیکھیے تھمے
نے ہاتھ باگ پر ہے نہ پا ہے رکاب میں

Rau mein hai rakhsh-e-umr, kahaan dekhiye thame,
Ne haath baag par hai, na paa hai rikaab mein.

My rendering: The horse of life races onwards; who knows where it will stop? The reins are not in my hands, nor are my feet secure in the stirrups.

We behave as though we are directing our lives with precision, deciding where we will go and when we will arrive.

Yet so much is shaped by circumstances we neither anticipate nor control.

We remain responsible, however, for how we respond.

We cannot determine every circumstance, but we can choose whether uncertainty makes us bitter or more compassionate towards others navigating their own difficulties. We can decide whether disappointment obscures everything that remains meaningful.

And we can ask whether the future we have not reached should be allowed to consume the present we already possess.

Between faith and the unknown

I find myself returning to the Qur'anic reminder in Surah Al-Kahf (18:23–24), which cautions us against speaking of tomorrow as though it were guaranteed, without remembering Insha'Allah — if Allah wills.

I increasingly understand this as a reminder of the limits of human knowledge, not a reason to stop planning.

Faith does not remove the responsibility to make an effort. Nor does effort remove the need for humility.

I would like to say I approach uncertainty with complete patience, but that would not be true.

There are moments when I question decisions I have made, the direction my life has taken, and possibilities I may have lost along the way.

I am not perfect. I am still learning, questioning, making mistakes and trying to become better. Acceptance is sometimes easier to discuss than to practise.

Perhaps faith is not always about finding an explanation for what happens. Perhaps the absence of an explanation does not necessarily mean the absence of meaning.

And perhaps trusting Allah is not the belief that everything will happen as we wish, but the ability to continue acting responsibly even when we cannot know the outcome.

The life that happens while we wait

How much of my life have I spent preparing for a version of myself who does not yet exist?

The person who will finally be settled. Who will have achieved enough. Who will be ready to marry, establish a family, become a father and live without constantly wondering whether circumstances may change again.

But what about the person I am today?

Does he exist merely to prepare the ground for someone who might arrive in five or ten years?

There is something troubling about treating entire periods of our lives as waiting rooms.

We spend years believing the real story will begin when a particular difficulty is resolved.

But those years are not outside the story. They are the story.

The friendships we make, conversations that stay with us, books we read, love we offer, disappointments we survive and small acts of kindness we receive are not merely preparation for life.

They are life itself.

The knowledge I have gained is real. The experiences and relationships are real. The person I have become over seven years is real. None of that disappears because a date changes.

And still the practical consequences of uncertainty are real. Philosophy cannot resolve every obstacle, and acceptance does not require pretending that missed opportunities do not hurt.

Perhaps maturity is learning to hold both truths without allowing either to erase the other.

And if tomorrow never looks the way I imagined?

I still want the things I have hoped for.

Meaningful work. Somewhere to call home. Someone with whom to share ordinary life. Perhaps marriage, children, and the opportunity to become a father whose presence offers reassurance in an uncertain world.

But perhaps I need to become less attached to the precise order in which these things must happen.

Perhaps the right person will not appear when everything becomes convenient. Perhaps opportunities will arrive during uncertainty rather than after it. Some plans may be abandoned, others revised, and a few may unfold in ways I cannot anticipate.

Accepting an unfinished future is not the same as giving up on it.

If I am fortunate enough to grow old, I doubt I will measure my life solely by the dates upon which milestones were achieved.

I imagine I will remember people. Conversations that changed my understanding. Moments when someone made me feel understood. Perhaps the person I loved, the family we built, the children I watched grow into lives of their own.

And perhaps the years when nothing seemed certain, but I continued trying anyway.

I wonder whether I will regret the plans that failed, or the years I spent so consumed by those plans that I forgot to appreciate what was happening around me.

Perhaps life is not about arriving at a place where uncertainty finally disappears.

Perhaps it is about learning to love, to hope, to build, to lose, to begin again and to remain human through circumstances that never stop changing.

I still hope to find stability. But I do not want to spend the entirety of my present waiting for the future to give my existence meaning.

The future, however beautiful we imagine it, remains a possibility.

The present is the only part of life we are actually living.

After all, what is a life worth if we spend all of it preparing to begin?
