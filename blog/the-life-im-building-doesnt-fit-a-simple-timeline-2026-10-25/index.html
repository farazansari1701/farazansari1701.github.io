The Life I'm Building Doesn't Fit a Simple Timeline

On living between India and Britain, family expectations, unfinished ambitions, and learning to make peace with a life that is still taking shape.

Faraz AnsariPersonal perspectives

10 minute read

There is a question that seems to follow people who have moved away from home: Are you settled yet?

It sounds simple enough. Almost considerate. A question asked by people who want to know whether life is treating you well.

But I have never been entirely sure what being settled is supposed to mean.

Does it mean having a job? A place to live? A certain amount of money? Getting married? Knowing exactly what the next five years will look like?

Or does it mean arriving at a point where you no longer feel that you are constantly trying to become someone?

I have been living in the United Kingdom since 2019. Seven years is a substantial part of a person's life. Long enough for a country that was once unfamiliar to become part of your everyday existence. Long enough for the person who arrived to grow into someone with different responsibilities, experiences and questions.

And yet, seven years does not necessarily mean having everything figured out.

I come from Varanasi, and I grew up in Mussoorie. Those places remain part of how I understand myself. Cambridge became part of my story when I came to Britain to study at Anglia Ruskin University. Then came London, work, independence, and the responsibility of building a life increasingly shaped by my own decisions.

On paper, there is a neat sequence to it.

India. Cambridge. London. Education. Career.

The sort of progression that looks convincing when presented in a biography.

What that sequence cannot tell you is what it means to be the person living through it.

A professional biography has no room for uncertainty. It rarely mentions the expectations waiting outside working hours, the decisions you have not yet made, or the strange experience of being proud of how far you have come while remaining unsure about what comes next.

Perhaps that is why I find the idea of being settled so difficult to define.

The different lives people expect us to build

Family conversations have a way of returning to certain subjects. Marriage is one of them.

I understand that, for many families, marriage represents stability, companionship and the reassurance that someone they care about is building a future.

But understanding the importance of something does not mean being ready to organise your entire life around it.

There is a particular exhaustion in repeatedly having to consider a decision whose importance you recognise, but whose timing may not match everything else happening in your life.

I do not want to dismiss the expectations of people who care about me. Neither do I want to treat my own hopes, responsibilities and uncertainties as though they are somehow less important.

Both things can be true.

You can love your family and still need the freedom to make decisions in your own time.

You can respect the life others imagine for you without being entirely certain it is the life you are ready to live.

And you can want companionship without believing that marriage is an answer to every unfinished part of your existence.

Sometimes I wonder whether the difficulty is not that people expect too much, but that we are all working with different ideas of what a good life should look like.

Perhaps none of us is particularly skilled at recognising how complicated another person's decisions can be.

What work teaches me, and what it cannot

My professional life exists in a world of plans, campaigns, deadlines, technology, data and measurable outcomes.

Working in digital marketing has taught me to think about problems, processes and possibilities. There is often a reason behind a result, something to investigate, something to improve, another approach worth testing.

I like the idea that difficult problems can be examined rather than merely accepted.

But life is not a marketing campaign.

There is no dashboard that can tell me whether I am becoming the person I want to be. No reliable performance indicator for belonging. No perfectly defined conversion point at which uncertainty becomes stability.

The things that matter most to us are often the hardest to measure.

How do you measure whether you have become more patient?

How do you know whether you have been fair to someone whose perspective differs from yours?

When does ambition become an endless dissatisfaction with the present?

And at what point does planning for a better future prevent you from appreciating the life you are already living?

I do not ask these questions because I have discovered the answers.

I ask them because they concern the kind of person I am trying to become, beyond any professional achievement.

The soil we grow in

There is a couplet by Allama Iqbal that seems particularly meaningful when thinking about unfinished lives:

نہیں ہے نا امید اقبالؔ اپنی کشتِ ویراں سے

ذرا نم ہو تو یہ مٹی بہت زرخیز ہے ساقی

Nahin hai na-umeed Iqbal apni kisht-e-veeran se,

Zara nam ho to ye mitti bahut zarkhez hai saqi.

My own English rendering would be:

Iqbal has not lost hope in his seemingly barren field; with a little nourishment, even this soil holds the promise of growth.

Iqbal's original poetic concerns reach beyond any one individual's career or personal circumstances. I do not want to reduce his vision to a convenient motivational quotation.

But the image of mitti—soil—interests me for another reason.

I have roots in one part of India, memories of growing up in another, and a life that has continued to develop in Britain.

So which soil am I supposed to belong to?

Perhaps that is the wrong question.

Perhaps we are not required to choose a single place as the complete explanation of who we are.

Mussoorie does not become less important because I studied in Cambridge. Varanasi does not disappear from my identity because I work in London. Nor does living in Britain mean I must remain permanently defined by the person I was when I first arrived.

Each place belongs to a different part of my story.

The challenge is learning how to carry those parts without feeling obliged to turn them into one uncomplicated identity.

With a visit to India ahead of me, that question feels particularly relevant.

I am returning to a place that belongs to my past and remains connected to my present. But I am not the same person who left India in 2019.

That is not necessarily a loss.

It is what happens when a person continues to live, encounter new perspectives, make mistakes and learn.

Faith, and the person I am not yet

Islam is another important part of how I understand these questions.

One of the things I value in my faith is the invitation to reflect: not only on the world around me, but on my own conduct and responsibilities.

The Qur'an, in Surah Al-Hujurat (49:13), speaks of humanity being made into different peoples and tribes so that they may know one another. It also places moral worth above inherited distinctions of identity.

The verse carries a religious meaning that deserves to be understood in its own context. But it also leaves me with something personal to consider.

If I value the dignity of human beings, how consistently do I extend that dignity to people who disagree with me?

If I believe in compassion, am I always compassionate?

If I speak about justice and fairness, how willing am I to examine my own behaviour?

I know that I do not always live up to the standards I admire.

I am not perfect. I am a sinner. I make mistakes, and I am still learning.

I would not want my writing to become a performance of goodness, as though being able to explain a principle makes me an example of it.

Nor do I want to use my imperfections as an excuse to remain unchanged.

There is a difference between accepting that I am human and becoming comfortable with every one of my shortcomings.

For me, faith is not an argument that I have finished learning. It is one of the reasons I feel I should continue.

And that includes learning from people who do not share every belief I hold.

I can find questions worth examining in philosophy, moral courage in history, and expressions of human experience in poetry without pretending that all these traditions agree with one another.

I want to be able to appreciate what is meaningful, question what I do not understand, and remain conscious of my own limitations.

The life happening between the plans

I sometimes think there is an unfair expectation that adulthood should eventually become a series of completed tasks.

Finish your education. Find work. Establish yourself. Get married. Make plans. Achieve stability.

Each milestone has its place. There is nothing wrong with wanting any of them.

But the way we speak about these stages can make the periods between them seem like waiting rooms, as though our lives only become meaningful once the next decision has been made.

I do not want to look back on my years in Britain and remember them only as preparation for some future moment when everything finally fell into place.

I studied here. I began building a career here. I have developed interests, changed perspectives, encountered ideas and continued trying to understand my place in the world.

Those years are not an introduction to my real life.

They are my life.

And perhaps that is something I need to remember more often.

There will always be another professional ambition, another personal decision, another uncertainty about the future.

There may always be people who imagine a different timetable for me.

I cannot expect every part of my life to satisfy everyone else's understanding of progress.

Nor can I claim to have worked out exactly what happiness, stability or success should mean for me.

I am still trying to find that balance between appreciating where I am and working towards where I hope to be.

Between independence and responsibility.

Between the home I came from and the life I am building.

Between what I believe and how faithfully I manage to practise it.

And between the person I am today and the person I hope I might become.

Perhaps being settled is not the same as being finished.

I live in Britain, but India remains part of me. I have built a professional life, but my ambitions are still developing. I have faith, but I am learning. I value human connection, but I do not always know how best to navigate its expectations.

None of these things makes my life incomplete.

They make it a life that is still unfolding.

I do not have an answer for every question about what comes next.

But I am here, making decisions, sometimes getting them wrong, learning from what I can, and trying to build something worthwhile.

For now, I think that deserves to count as living.

Not merely preparing to live.
