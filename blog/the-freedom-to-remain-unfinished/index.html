The Freedom to Remain Unfinished

On love, expectations, and the difficulty of understanding a person who is still becoming.

Faraz AnsariPersonal perspectives

Today was productive, but it was not simple.

There was a sense of achievement alongside uncertainty; moments of feeling understood alongside feelings that remained unresolved. I cannot honestly reduce the day to one mood. Nor am I convinced that I should.

Something can go well without putting everything else in order.

Perhaps that sounds obvious. Yet I wonder how often we deny ourselves that possibility. We look for a description that will make a day, a relationship, or a person coherent. Happy or unhappy. Close or distant. Certain or confused. We want the comfort of knowing which story we are in.

But I could recognise progress today without knowing quite what to do with everything I felt.

That contradiction seems worth staying with.

Not because every uncertainty contains some profound revelation, but because rushing to resolve it might mean overlooking something true.

And it leads me towards a question about love: can we allow someone to be understood without expecting them to become completely predictable?

When knowing becomes expecting

There is something deeply appealing about being known. The possibility that another person might understand what matters to us, recognise a worry without dismissing it, or listen without immediately trying to correct us. I can see why that kind of attention makes love meaningful.

But I wonder where understanding ends and expectation begins.

To know someone is to develop a sense of them: how they communicate, what they value, what they might need. Some of that familiarity is necessary. Without it, closeness would mean beginning again with a stranger every day.

Still, there is a difference between recognising a person and believing we have reached the end of knowing them.

The sentence you have changed interests me because it can contain such different things. It might be an expression of concern. It might be an honest recognition that something important has shifted. But it can also carry an accusation, as though the person we first understood had agreed to remain that way.

I am not suggesting that every change is growth, or that every disappointed expectation is unreasonable. Honesty matters. Consideration matters. A promise cannot simply be dismissed because the person who made it now feels differently.

Yet I wonder whether we sometimes ask for something beyond these things. We ask another person to keep confirming our original understanding of them. And perhaps we make the same demand of ourselves.

Once I have described myself in a certain way, must I defend that description indefinitely? Can I reconsider an opinion without treating the earlier version of myself as a fraud? Can I discover that something matters to me more—or less—than I once believed?

I would like to think that sincerity does not require permanent certainty. But I also know how easily the language of change could become a way of avoiding an uncomfortable explanation. That is part of the difficulty. There is no phrase generous enough to remove the need for judgement.

The contradictions I cannot place elsewhere

It is easier to notice a gap between somebody else’s words and actions than to examine the tensions within my own thinking.

Today, it was a contradiction in my own thinking that challenged me.

I do not have to explain its private particulars to recognise the larger question it raises: what should I do when the things I want do not fit neatly together?

Closeness and independence both matter to me. So do being understood and being free to form my own view. Love can feel meaningful without making its expectations simple.

These desires need not be enemies. But neither can I assume they will arrange themselves without effort.

Wanting freedom does not answer the question of what I owe another person. Wanting reassurance does not tell me how much reassurance it is fair to ask for. Feeling uncertain does not make every decision impossible, though it may make a decision harder to inhabit.

I find myself wondering whether the aim should be to remove these contradictions or to become more honest about them.

There is a temptation to present a tidier self: someone whose principles, feelings and choices all point in the same direction. It would certainly be easier to explain. But would it be more truthful?

I do not want to make complexity an excuse. A person can describe themselves as complicated and still owe someone an apology. Uncertainty does not make the consequences of our actions disappear.

Perhaps the more difficult task is to acknowledge both things at once: that I am still trying to understand myself, and that other people should not have to bear unlimited confusion while I do.

A little less force

A couplet by Mir Taqi Mir offers an image through which to consider this delicacy:

لے سانس بھی آہستہ کہ نازک ہے بہت کام
آفاق کی اس کارگہ شیشہ گری کا

Le saans bhi aahista ki naazuk hai bahut kaam,
Aafaaq ki is kaargah-e-shishagari ka.

My rendering: Even breathe gently: so delicate is the work of this glassmaking workshop of the world.

What interests me in this image is the care it invites. I take it not as a reason to avoid difficult truths, but as a reminder to consider how we approach them.

A question can make room for an answer, or it can leave someone feeling that the answer has already been decided. Familiarity can help us listen, but it might also persuade us that listening is no longer necessary.

I wonder what it would mean to bring a little less force to the effort of understanding.

Not less attention. Not less honesty.

Simply less certainty that my first interpretation must be the correct one.

That seems especially important when the subject is another person’s inner life. I can notice what someone says or does. I can explain how it affects me. But knowing a person closely does not give me complete access to everything behind their words.

The same restraint might be useful when I turn towards myself.

An unresolved feeling does not always need an immediate verdict. Sometimes the most honest description available is that it is unresolved.

What freedom asks in return

I chose the freedom to disagree without hostility as something worth thinking about today. The more I consider it, the more I see its relevance beyond public arguments.

Would I still feel understood by someone who listened carefully and then disagreed with me?

I hope so. But the question is more demanding than it first appears.

It asks whether I want attention or agreement. Whether I can distinguish a challenge to my reasoning from a rejection of who I am. Whether another person can remain close to me without becoming an echo.

And it asks something in return.

If I want room to reconsider my opinions, I must be willing to encounter changes in other people that I did not anticipate. If I want my uncertainty treated with care, I cannot reserve patience only for uncertainty that is convenient to me.

None of this means accepting every action or remaining in every relationship. Freedom includes the ability to recognise a boundary. Understanding why something happens does not oblige us to accept its consequences indefinitely.

But there may be a way of holding a boundary without claiming to have reduced the whole person to what disappointed us.

I do not find that easy to define. I am wary of making it sound easier than it is.

Perhaps this is where freedom and responsibility genuinely meet: not in the absence of expectations, but in the willingness to examine them, explain them, and recognise that another person has a life beyond the role they occupy in ours.

Must everything be understood?

Another question remains with me: is happiness more important than understanding?

I cannot settle it by choosing one and dismissing the other.

There is something unsatisfying about the idea of happiness that depends on refusing to look closely. Yet I am equally uncertain about an understanding that requires every feeling to be examined until nothing can simply be experienced.

Must I fully explain why something matters before allowing it to matter?

Can a good moment remain good even when it does not answer the larger questions?

Today, feeling heard did not remove uncertainty. Achievement did not settle everything else. I am trying to resist the conclusion that this made either of those things inadequate.

Perhaps I ask too much of a single experience when I expect it to resolve the whole of life.

The same question could be asked of love.

It may be meaningful without supplying a final account of who I am or what my life is for. It may offer companionship in uncertainty rather than an escape from it.

That possibility appeals to me, though it does not answer every question about trust, distance or expectation. Nor should it be used to romanticise a relationship that leaves someone perpetually unsure of where they stand.

There must be a distinction between room to grow and an absence of care. I am still interested in where that distinction lies.

For now, I do not have a neat conclusion about what makes love meaningful, how much understanding is enough, or when an expectation becomes a demand another person cannot reasonably fulfil.

What I have is a day that held achievement and unease, connection and unanswered questions. Neither side made the other untrue.

I would like to bring that same patience to the way I understand a person—including myself. To take what is present seriously without pretending that nothing remains to be discovered.

I would like to be understood. I am less sure that I would like to be finished.
