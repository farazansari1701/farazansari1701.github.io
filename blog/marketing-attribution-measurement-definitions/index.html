Marketing attribution: agree the measurement definitions

A practical framework for defining what a campaign report measures before deciding what its attribution means.

Faraz AnsariSenior Digital Marketing Executive
23 October 2026
6 minute read

  2. Agree what the denominator contains

  4. Fix the time boundary before comparing periods

Marketing attribution assigns credit for an important action to the touchpoints that preceded it. Before comparing models, define the action, the people or events being counted, the reporting period and the attribution window. My proposed starting point is a short measurement agreement: everyone should understand what the report can answer before using it to change a campaign.

This is educational guidance, rather than an account of a measured OKI campaign. My public marketing responsibilities provide professional context, but the examples below are hypothetical. They illustrate a review method without presenting employer data or claiming that a particular channel caused an outcome.

Good attribution definitions also depend on a coherent destination. My landing-page clarity checklist examines the promise, evidence and action awaiting each visitor.

An answer-first article structure can help present measurement conclusions to readers who need the practical decision before the technical explanation.

Start with the decision, then define the outcome

A useful attribution question is specific: which recorded touchpoints receive credit for submitted enquiry forms during this reporting period? “Which channel works best?” leaves too much unspecified. It can mean acquisition volume, engagement, sales acceptance or revenue, each requiring different evidence and a different comparison.

Write down the action before opening the dashboard. A form submission, a booked meeting and an accepted opportunity are separate milestones. A click on an email address shows contact intent; it does not establish that a message was sent or a conversation occurred. Keep those distinctions visible in the report title and commentary.

For a closer distinction between a counted interaction and a completed request, see my GA4 enquiry-event audit.

Google’s key event guidance explains how an important collected event can be marked for reporting. That designation expresses its importance to the business. It does not independently validate the real-world outcome behind the event. Define a separate confirmation source when the decision depends on an actual enquiry or sale.

Agree what the denominator contains

Attribution distributes credit, while a rate compares an outcome with a defined base. Keep those jobs separate. If a report includes a conversion rate, specify whether its denominator is sessions, users, clicks or eligible records. A label such as “enquiry rate per session” is easier to interpret than an unexplained percentage.

For example, imagine a fictional campaign with repeated visits from the same person. A count of sessions and a count of people describe different things even when both are accurate. Do not combine a user-based numerator with a session-based denominator unless that calculation is intentional and clearly explained. Also record whether repeated actions are counted separately.

This extends the distinction in my guide to B2B lead quality beyond cost per lead. That article asks what a lead means commercially. An attribution review asks which recorded interactions receive credit for the chosen milestone. Neither question can substitute for the other.

Keep acquisition scope and attribution scope separate

Before comparing channels, identify the dimension used to group the data. Google documents different meanings for first-user, session and event-scoped channel groups. A first-user view describes initial acquisition, while a session view describes session acquisition. Event-scoped attribution answers another question about credit for an action.

I would put the dimension name beside every exported channel table. If colleagues compare a first-user acquisition report with an event-scoped attribution report, explain the scope difference before interpreting any disagreement. Different answers can reflect different questions, rather than a tracking fault. Matching labels such as “Paid Search” do not make the underlying populations equivalent.

Fix the time boundary before comparing periods

Record the reporting dates and property timezone. A London working day and a reporting day in another timezone may have different boundaries. Save the extraction time too, especially when the most recent period is still being processed. A partial day should not be presented as a completed-day decline.

The attribution lookback window is another boundary. It determines how far before the action an eligible touchpoint may be considered. Google’s attribution settings documentation explains the relevant controls. Record the setting actually used rather than assuming that every platform or report uses the same window.

For a proposed review, I would compare complete periods using consistent filters and settings, then document any unavoidable differences. Where the buying cycle extends beyond the reporting period, distinguish the date of an interaction from the date of the outcome. This makes late outcomes easier to discuss without retrospectively inventing a campaign success story.

Compare models without treating credit as causation

Once the definitions are stable, compare attribution models against the same selected action and audience. Google’s attribution models report supports examining how credit changes between models. A shift in allocation is useful evidence about the reporting method, but it is not proof of incremental business impact.

Consider a hypothetical path involving an email, a search visit and an enquiry form. A model may allocate credit differently across those recorded interactions. That does not reveal what would have happened without the email or search activity. Answering that causal question needs an appropriate evaluation design, with its own assumptions and limitations.

I would therefore separate observations from decisions: record the credit allocation, note what is missing, then state the next investigation. Avoid cutting a channel solely because one model assigns it less credit. Check the consistency of the result and whether the measured action is sufficiently close to the business objective.

Check identity and handovers before explaining gaps

A reporting discrepancy may begin with records or definitions. Review campaign naming and UTM conventions, duplicate contacts, missing identifiers and the relationship between a website action and its CRM record. My CRM data quality review provides a practical starting point for checking those foundations before automating decisions.

The same care applies when handing an enquiry to another team. Document who confirms acceptance, where that status is stored and how corrections reach the report. These proposed checks complement my broader marketing automation reflections: connected journeys need clear ownership as well as functioning tools.

Questions to settle before the next review

Which attribution model should I use?

Choose a model that fits the reporting question and available platform capabilities. First document the event, scope, filters and time boundaries. A sophisticated model cannot repair an ambiguous outcome definition. Keep the chosen model consistent long enough to make a meaningful comparison, while recording changes explicitly.

Can contact clicks be reported as leads?

Report them as contact intent unless a verified enquiry outcome supports a stronger label. Keep clicks, submissions, accepted leads and opportunities separate. If only click data exists, say so and propose how a confirmed outcome could be recorded without exposing personal information.

What should the review leave behind?

Keep a concise measurement note containing the decision, event definition, counting basis, dimension scope, period, timezone, lookback setting, model, exclusions and unresolved gaps.
