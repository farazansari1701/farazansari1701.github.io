I Think, I Question, Therefore I Am

But who is this 'I' that thinks? And why am I conscious of being alive at all?

Sometimes, the strangest thing about life is not that it ends.

It is that, for a little while, I am here to experience it.

I can see the world, hear sounds, remember things that happened years ago, imagine things that have not happened yet, and think about things that may never exist.

Somewhere within all of this, there is something I call me.

But what exactly is that?

Am I my body? My memories? My beliefs? My emotions? Or am I something observing them all?

Why am I experiencing this particular moment, through these particular eyes, as this particular person? Why am I me, rather than someone else? And what does it even mean to ask that?

The world existed before I knew it. It will continue after me. Yet everything I have ever understood about it has depended upon being here to experience it.

Even when I try to imagine my absence, I place myself somewhere within that imagination, observing a world without me.

But if I were truly absent, who would be there to notice?

How can I understand what it means not to exist when everything I have ever understood has depended upon my existence?

Who is the person inside my thoughts?

René Descartes famously proposed: Cogito, ergo sum.

I think, therefore I am.

He doubted what he could doubt and found certainty in the existence of the thinking subject.

I find that fascinating. But who is this I that thinks?

When a thought enters my mind, did I create it, or did it simply appear?

If I decide what to think next, where did that decision come from? Can I choose my next thought before I have thought it?

Sometimes an old conversation returns without invitation. A face. A feeling. A moment I had not consciously tried to remember.

Why does my mind preserve some memories and let others disappear? Who decides what stays?

If my thoughts arrive without my permission, am I their author or their observer?

Perhaps both. But who is doing the observing?

I can question a thought, recognise that it may be mistaken, and decide not to act upon it. Yet the act of questioning is itself another thought.

Am I attempting to understand consciousness by using the very thing I cannot step outside?

Is it not strange that the mind tries to understand itself while remaining within the limits of its own understanding?

Am I the same person I was yesterday?

I remember being younger. Some beliefs that once seemed obvious no longer persuade me. Some ambitions no longer matter. Even my understanding of old experiences has changed.

So am I still the same person?

If my body changes, memories fade and opinions evolve, what remains constant?

Is identity the story I tell myself about my past? And if I remember that past differently tomorrow, does my identity change?

If I forget someone I once loved, does the love cease to have existed?

If I become different from who I was, who is responsible for my earlier decisions?

I cannot erase their consequences merely because I have changed. But if change is impossible, what is the purpose of learning?

Perhaps I am neither entirely the person I was nor entirely someone new.

But why does yesterday's pain still feel like mine? Why does tomorrow's uncertainty already concern me? And why am I responsible for someone I have not yet become?

Am I experiencing reality, or my interpretation of it?

Everything I know about the world reaches me through my senses and the meanings I attach to them.

Two people can hear the same words and experience entirely different emotions. Neither must be dishonest.

So how much of what I call reality is interpretation?

When someone hurts me, am I describing their intention, my experience, or both?

When someone disappoints me, am I responding to what happened or to what I expected would happen?

I cannot enter another person's consciousness. I can listen, observe and learn, but I cannot experience the world from inside their mind.

How, then, can I claim to understand anyone completely?

And if I cannot, why am I sometimes so quick to judge?

Perhaps every person carries an entire world of memories, fears and hopes that I will never fully know.

What if being conscious is not just experiencing my own existence, but recognising that others are experiencing theirs?

What is time if I can never hold on to it?

I speak of the past as though it exists behind me and the future as though it is waiting ahead.

But where is the present?

By the time I recognise a moment, has some of it not already passed?

The past reaches me through memory and consequences. The future exists for me through expectation and imagination.

Am I living in a moment that continually becomes a memory?

Why can an hour feel endless while entire years seem to disappear when I look back? Why does waiting stretch time, while happiness sometimes makes it pass unnoticed?

Does a year full of experiences count for more than a quiet year? Have I lived more simply because I remember more?

I prepare for a future in which life will be different. But when it arrives, I begin preparing for another future.

Am I living my life, or continually preparing to live it?

Why does consciousness make loss possible?

There is a couplet by Obaidullah Aleem that I keep returning to.

ہے یوں بھی زیاں اور یوں بھی زیاں
جی جاؤ تو کیا مر جاؤ تو کیا

Hai yun bhi ziyaan aur yun bhi ziyaan,
Jee jaao toh kya, mar jaao toh kya.

My rendering: There is loss either way. What difference does it make whether one lives or dies?

I do not read this as proof that life is worthless. I hear a question that can arise when something through which we understood our existence loses its meaning.

Why can an absence continue to affect me long after a person or moment is gone?

Is grief the experience of remembering what I can no longer have? Why does the mind preserve what the world has taken away?

I can experience happiness, but also remember happiness that has ended. I can love, but also fear losing someone. I can imagine a future, but also fear that it will never arrive.

Does consciousness enrich life by allowing these possibilities, or burden it by making me anticipate their loss?

Perhaps both.

But why must every painful experience produce a lesson? Can something simply hurt, without needing to justify itself through the wisdom that follows?

I cannot accept that living and dying are interchangeable. While I am alive, possibilities remain: learning, repairing a mistake, loving, helping, seeking forgiveness.

Yet why am I so eager to assemble reasons for life to matter?

Should the value of being alive require an argument at all?

Why can I imagine death but not my own absence?

I know that I will die. I know the world will continue in ways I will never witness.

Yet I cannot truly imagine my own non-experience.

I can picture people continuing their lives without me. But in that picture, there is still an observer: me.

How can that be an accurate picture of my absence?

Is it death that troubles us, or uncertainty? Unfinished ambitions? Separation? The knowledge that there will be experiences we never have?

I understand mortality intellectually. But have I accepted it emotionally?

If I genuinely understood that my time is limited, would I become more generous or more anxious? Would I forgive more easily? Would I stop postponing what matters?

And why do I behave as though I have been promised another opportunity?

If Allah created me with consciousness, what am I to do with it?

As a Muslim, my questions eventually bring me back to Allah.

I believe my existence has a purpose beyond what I immediately observe. Yet believing that does not mean I understand everything about being alive.

In Surah Al-Mulk (67:2), Allah tells us He created death and life to test which of us is best in deeds.

Why do I measure my life through achievements when the verse directs attention towards conduct?

Why does recognition matter so much when I believe Allah knows what I do even when nobody else sees?

In Surah Ali 'Imran (3:190–191), the Qur'an draws attention to those who remember Allah and reflect upon creation.

Does being conscious of suffering make me responsible for responding to it? If I know my words can hurt someone, am I not responsible for how I use them?

In Surah Al-Mu'minun (23:115), Allah asks whether we thought we had been created without purpose and would not return to Him.

I find it striking that the verse confronts an assumption through a question.

Perhaps my difficulty is not that I have been given no answer about purpose. It is that I am still learning to live according to the answer I believe I have been given.

I am not perfect. I am a sinner, learning every day and trying to become better.

I know compassion matters, yet I sometimes struggle to understand people. I value humility, yet become attached to my opinions. I know life is temporary, yet worry about things as though they were permanent.

Why is there such a distance between knowing something and living according to it?

Is recognising that distance a failure of consciousness, or the beginning of moral growth?

Am I free, or a collection of circumstances?

I did not choose when I was born, my earliest experiences, or the world that existed before I became conscious of it.

Yet I must make choices and take responsibility for them.

How much freedom do I truly possess?

My decisions are influenced by upbringing, memories, fears and hopes. Does freedom mean escaping every influence, or examining them and choosing how to respond?

If I understand why someone acted as they did, does that make their action acceptable?

Where does compassion end and accountability begin?

And if my intentions were good but my choices hurt someone, is understanding why I acted enough? Or must that understanding change what I do next?

What if thinking is preventing me from living?

What if my constant enquiry sometimes distances me from the experience I want to understand?

Am I so concerned with the meaning of happiness that I overlook moments when I am happy?

Must every feeling have a reason? Must every experience teach me something? Must every relationship reveal a truth about human nature?

Can I simply be?

Can I enjoy a conversation without examining it, appreciate beauty without explaining it, love without completely defining love?

I do not want to spend my life merely collecting answers. Nor do I want to accept everything without examination.

I want to understand, but I want to live. I want to think, but also to feel. I want to question what I believe, but have the courage to act upon what I believe is right.

How much questioning helps me live more consciously, and when does it become a way of avoiding life?

I think. I question. Therefore, I am.

I began by wondering what it means to be alive.

I still do not understand why consciousness feels like one continuous experience when everything about me changes. Why I inhabit this particular mind. Why the past can feel present, or the future affect me before it arrives.

I do not understand why I become attached to things I cannot keep forever, or why I know I am mortal and still behave as though time is unlimited.

And perhaps most strangely, I do not fully understand what I mean by I.

Yet I am here, thinking about being here.

I think, therefore I am.

I question, therefore I recognise how much I do not understand.

But does awareness of my ignorance make me wiser? Does questioning my behaviour make me more compassionate? Does knowing life is temporary help me value it?

Not necessarily.

I can be conscious and still behave carelessly. I can question and still refuse an uncomfortable answer. I can know time is limited and still waste it.

Perhaps consciousness itself guarantees nothing about who I become.

I return once more to Aleem's question: Jee jaao toh kya, mar jaao toh kya?

While I am here, I can learn, reconsider, love, make mistakes, seek forgiveness and change. Yet listing those possibilities does not explain why consciousness exists.

Before I became conscious, the world existed without my knowing it. One day, my experience of this world will end. And somewhere between those realities is this uncertain thing I call my life.

Why have I been given the experience of being me?

And what if the responsibility of consciousness is not merely to know that I exist, but to consider how I exist?

I do not know.

I think. I question. I am.

But am I truly living consciously, or merely conscious of being alive?

And if those are different things, how much of my life have I already lived without understanding the difference?
