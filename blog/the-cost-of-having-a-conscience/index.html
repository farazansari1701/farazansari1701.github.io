The Cost of Having a Conscience

On work, insecurity and the distance between principle and practice

Faraz AnsariPersonal perspectives
27 October 2026
7 minute read
Inside this article

    Some questions sound manageable when we first ask them. It is only later that we discover how much of ordinary life they contain.

    Today was busy and mentally exhausting. One thing I returned to was a conversation with a friend about ethics: the principles described in books, the expectations of a capitalist society, and how realistic it is to live by those principles. I cannot claim the conversation produced a revelation, nor do I want to turn it into an invented exchange of arguments. What I have is the question itself, and the feeling that it deserves more attention than a quick answer.

    There is a certain comfort in an ethical principle written on a page. It can be precise. It can tell us to act fairly, be honest, consider the consequences of our decisions, or respect another person's dignity. We can read it, agree with it and feel that the difficult part has been done.

    Then we have to live.

    In everyday life, a choice rarely arrives without circumstances attached. Time matters. Money matters. The fear of disappointing someone matters. So does the possibility of losing an opportunity that may not come again. What appears obvious in a classroom can become complicated when the person making the decision has something to lose.

    I am not suggesting that hardship makes every choice acceptable. If that were true, ethics would disappear whenever life became inconvenient. But I do wonder whether we are too quick to demand moral courage from people without asking what that courage might cost them.

    Perhaps this is where my questions about work–life balance begin.

    We speak about balance as if it were something a person could arrange by becoming a little more organised. Yet the amount of time we can call our own is not always entirely ours to decide. Work is not simply an activity that fills the day. It can provide purpose, independence, stability and the chance to build something. It can also make demands on the time we might otherwise give to friendship, reflection, rest or the parts of ourselves that have no economic value.

    Consider a hypothetical choice: someone is asked to make themselves available beyond their agreed hours. One person may feel free to refuse. Another may worry about what refusing might mean for their position. The ethical question looks similar from a distance; the freedom to answer it may be very different.

    I keep thinking about that difference. How much of a moral decision belongs to character, and how much depends on the circumstances in which character is being tested?

    In The Theory of Moral Sentiments, published in 1759, Adam Smith begins with a striking resistance to reducing human beings to selfishness: “How selfish soever man may be supposed”. He goes on to consider our capacity to care about the fortunes of others, even without a personal reward. This is the same Adam Smith so often associated with the development of modern economic thought. His concern with sympathy complicates the easy assumption that economic life and moral life occupy separate worlds.

    But the fact that people can feel compassion does not mean the arrangements around them will always reward it.

    Karl Marx approached the problem differently. In Capital, his discussion of the working day considers not only the physical need for rest but the time required for a person's intellectual and social life. He saw limits on working hours as the subject of conflict between competing economic interests. I do not have to turn his analysis into an answer to every modern question to recognise the one that remains relevant: who gets to decide how much of a person's life work may claim?

    Smith makes me consider what we are capable of feeling for one another. Marx makes me consider the conditions under which that concern is expected to operate. They do not offer interchangeable arguments. Nor do I think I have to choose a complete worldview tonight to learn from the tension between them.

    I noticed insecurity in my thoughts about human behaviour today. Ambition can be generous: it can be the desire to support people, improve one's circumstances or attempt something difficult. But insecurity can make competition feel less like a choice than an obligation. I cannot say what motivates any particular person. I can only ask whether fear sometimes makes us less willing to behave as we believe we should.

    There is another difficulty. It is tempting to place every moral contradiction at the feet of a system. Then nobody has to answer for what they choose. It is just as tempting to insist that everything comes down to individual character. Then we never have to examine the rules, incentives or inequalities that shape those choices.

    Neither explanation seems sufficient to me.

    An organisation can make room for fairer decisions; an individual can still choose poorly within it. A person can act with care under pressure; that does not make the pressure just. Perhaps ethics asks us to look in both directions: at ourselves and at the arrangements we have learned to call normal.

    There is a couplet by Mirza Ghalib that feels especially relevant to these questions:

    بس کہ دشوار ہے ہر کام کا آساں ہونا
    آدمی کو بھی میسر نہیں انساں ہونا

    Bas ki dushvaar hai har kaam ka aasaan hona,
    Aadmi ko bhi muyassar nahin insaan hona.

    My rendering: How difficult it is for anything to become easy; even being human does not guarantee that one learns to be humane.

    The distinction between aadmi and insaan is what stays with me.

    To be born human is one thing.

    To become humane is another.

    Ghalib’s words do not offer an economic argument. They point towards something more fundamental: the distance between our existence as human beings and the qualities we associate with humanity.

    Perhaps ethics lives within that distance.

    It is easy to identify cruelty in others while overlooking smaller injustices in ourselves.

    I also thought about disagreement today. There are times when I choose not to express an opinion, and I did so today without describing the circumstances. I cannot honestly say whether that silence came from thoughtfulness, uncertainty or something else. But I am interested in the question it raises. When is restraint a form of respect, and when does it become a way of avoiding an uncomfortable truth?

    A view different from my own gave me something to reconsider, though I do not want to assign words or beliefs to another person. I would like to think disagreement can remain a conversation rather than becoming a contest that somebody must win. That matters to friendship as much as it matters to public life. What makes a friendship endure if not, at least in part, the possibility of being different without being discarded?

    And perhaps that brings me to the question of whether people can change.

    I want to believe that a person can become more thoughtful, more compassionate and more willing to acknowledge a mistake. Yet change is harder to recognise than the declaration that we intend to change. It becomes visible in how we act when the easier choice is available, and in whether we are prepared to question assumptions that have served us well.

    There is no neat solution waiting at the end of this day. I am still trying to understand how to protect a life outside work without dismissing the value of work itself; how to think about inequality without treating individuals as powerless; and how to expect ethical behaviour without overlooking the price it can carry.

    Perhaps the question is not simply whether ethics can survive in a capitalist society.

    It is whether we are willing to build lives, relationships and institutions in which doing the right thing is not always the hardest thing to afford.

    Sources & context

    1. Adam Smith — The Theory of Moral Sentiments, Part I, Section I, Chapter I — Smith's opening discussion of sympathy and concern for others.
    2. Karl Marx — Capital, Volume I, Chapter 10: The Working-Day — Discussion of limits on working time and of time beyond work.
    3. Mirza Ghalib — bas-ki dushvaar hai har kaam kaa aasaan honaa (ghazal) — Original Urdu couplet and its attribution; the English rendering in the essay is the author's interpretation.
